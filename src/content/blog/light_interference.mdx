Здоров! Today, I was surfing the internet and spontaneously decided to replicate the double-slit experiment. After some searching around, I came across this [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kKdaRJ3vAmA). I had almost everything I needed except for the 38-gauge wire. I did have an old Ethernet cable with a 24-gauge strand inside—it was way thicker, but I figured I'd give it a shot anyway.

I tried replicating the setup from the video, but of course, it didn't work out. So, I pivoted to a similar experiment mentioned in the video that I honestly had never heard of before: **x-y component independent diffraction.** It's a variation of [Fraunhofer diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraunhofer_diffraction) where you fire a laser through a small rectangular hole, and it creates a beautiful cross pattern.

Everything looked great in person, but when I tried to take a photo of it, my phone just captured a blurry red dot. Luckily, with a bit of help from AI, I jumped into **Pro Mode**, tweaked my camera settings manually, and managed to capture these beautiful photos!

Honestly, it’s not exactly what you expect after seeing those perfect textbook animations. It still looks amazing and feels highly counter-intuitive from a day-to-day perspective (even if science doesn't call it that), but it’s just different in real life. 

However, while I was tweaking the **slit size** and **position** of the laser, I found out that when I aimed the laser slightly downward, the interference lines suddenly became incredibly clear:

<img src="https://i.imgur.com/Pugff7i.jpeg" alt="photos show double-slit laser point down experiment outcome" />

What is actually happening here? It turns out I accidentally stumbled upon a classic optical phenomenon called [Lloyd's Mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyd%27s_mirror) combined with grazing incidence reflection.

Because I aimed the laser at a **very flat angle** against the table, the surface began acting like a highly reflective mirror. The light traveling directly from the light source (laser) collided with the light reflecting off the table. These two paths overlapped right on the table's surface, creating a perfectly compressed, ultra-sharp interference pattern. It basically turned my desk into part of the physics experiment!

I also captured a photo of what appeared on the table surface at the exact point of reflection. As you can see, it forms these perfectly straight lines:

All photos were captured with good old shaking hands. Here is **how to tweak the camera** settings: First, you should balance the **ISO** (the camera sensor's sensitivity to light) and the **shutter speed** (the amount of time the sensor is exposed to light). Once those are balanced, adjust the **manual focus**. Once you have a razor-sharp view, you can **take the photo**.

In summary, the behavior of light is strange but incredibly interesting.
